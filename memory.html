THE PROBLEM · knowledge scatters

Every answer lives in somebody's head.

How did we close that deal? Why did that client leave? What did we decide in March? Every business runs on answers like these, and almost none are written where anyone can find them.

So the team re-solves what it solved last year, and when someone leaves, what they knew leaves too. Your AI has the same problem, worse. Every session opens knowing nothing about your clients or last week's decision. You paste the context again.

The answers a business runs on live in heads and inboxes: re-derived every week, lost with every departure, and re-explained to the assistant every session.

THE MECHANISM

Plain files, and the rules that decide where everything goes.

Most tools give you a store and leave you the hard part: where a thing goes. This method makes that mechanical.

Plain files you can open

Markdown in folders. No database, no vendor, nothing to host. You can read the whole memory, and every change is a diff.

00-core/       the rules it boots with
10-memory/     what the business knows
  episodic/    what happened, dated
  semantic/    facts, clients, decisions
  procedural/  how you do things
  inbox.md     raw capture, nothing lost
20-ventures/   offers · pipeline · clients
40-artifacts/  what you actually read

The real tree, not a diagram. A few hundred lines in play.

One boot file

One short file says what is true right now. The rest is read on demand.

Claims kept apart from artifacts

A claim can be true or false and can go stale. An artifact just becomes irrelevant. Filed apart by what a thing is, never what it is about.

A placement rule any agent follows

One procedure, top to bottom, stop at the first match. Nobody needs judgment to file.

A consolidation review

A review someone actually runs: the inbox gets placed, duplicates merged, stale claims marked. Without it, any wiki rots.

The mechanism: plain markdown files, one boot file, claims filed apart from artifacts, a placement rule an agent runs without judgment, and a consolidation review that keeps the memory from rotting.

PROOF · including the half that went against me

I measured it, and published the result my own design lost.

The benchmarkPUBLISHED · 498 QUESTIONS · THREE ARMS

Same questions, same model, same scoring in every arm. Organized plain files plus search answered 78 out of every 100. The same files in one flat folder answered 75. One-shot retrieval over embeddings answered 55. At that size the first two are not separable, and the organized one cost 27 percent more. The win is the files and the loop, not my tree.

The audit4,480 TRANSCRIPTS · MY OWN

I audited 4,480 of my own session transcripts. One security rule sat outside the thing it governed, so it reported success while permitting what it existed to prevent. Moving it inside took violations from 773 a day to 5.

THE QUESTIONS PEOPLE ASK

Five questions, before anyone starts.

Who feeds it?

Your people, inside the work they already do, plus the assistant they already use. Unclear items go to one inbox.

How much of my team's time does this cost?

Capture is a line at a time, inside the work. The real cost is the review, and it belongs to one owner.

What if we stop maintaining it?

It goes stale, exactly like a wiki. That is why the review ships inside the build.

We were going to hire someone for this.

A coordinator holding the company's memory is one more single brain, and it leaves when they leave.

We already have a wiki. We already use an assistant.

A wiki has no rule for where things go and no review, so it rots. An assistant chatting over your files is a reader. This is the structured memory underneath.
